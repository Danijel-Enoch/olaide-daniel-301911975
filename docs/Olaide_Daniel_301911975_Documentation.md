## 1. Selected organisation

**Harmony Digital Microfinance Bank (HDMB)** — a *simulated* Nigerian
microfinance bank, used consistently across all five portfolio activities.

* **Nature of the system.** HDMB holds deposits for individuals and small
  traders and moves money between them. Its core is a **double-entry ledger**:
  twelve branches across Lagos, Abuja, Ibadan, Port Harcourt, Enugu and Kano
  serve roughly 20,000 customers, but most activity arrives through
  self-service channels — USSD `*977#`, the mobile app, internet banking, ATMs
  and agent POS — rather than over a counter.
* **Users of the database.** Customers (self-service channels), branch tellers,
  POS agents, operations and reconciliation officers, credit officers, branch
  managers and head office, and auditors/regulator.
* **Major data stored.** Parties (customers with BVN and KYC tier, staff);
  reference data (branches, account products, channels); accounts with
  balances; transactions and their journal legs; the loan book and repayments;
  an append-only audit trail.
* **Key operations supported.** Open customers and accounts; transfer funds
  atomically; post cash deposits and withdrawals against a branch till; reverse
  a disputed transaction without destroying history; produce statements and a
  balance as at a past date; run operational reporting under load; serve the
  mobile app's home screen and rate limits; and survive the loss of a node.

HDMB does not exist and **no real data is used**: every row is generated by a
seeded script (`setseed(0.301911975)`), so the entire portfolio is reproducible.

Scale: 20,012 customers · 24,807 accounts · 274,795 transactions ·
549,590 ledger entries · 2,708 loans. The volume is deliberate — on a few dozen
rows PostgreSQL always chooses a sequential scan and no genuine

## 2. Portfolio 1 — Database Design and Modelling (7 marks)

Twelve relations in schema `hdmb`, implemented on PostgreSQL 18.6. The central
design decision is that **a transfer is not one row**: it is a header in `txn`
plus two or more rows in `ledger_entry` (debit and credit). This makes "money
is conserved" a database-level invariant that can be checked, makes a reversal
an insert rather than an update, and preserves the balance each account held at
the moment of posting.

* **Normalisation** is shown from an unnormalised teller spreadsheet through
  1NF → 2NF → 3NF → BCNF, with the functional dependencies that justify each
  decomposition, one documented BCNF exception, a 4NF note, and the two
  deliberate denormalisations (`account.balance`, `ledger_entry.balance_after`)
  with the test that keeps them honest.
* **Integrity layer:** 12 primary keys, 15 foreign keys, 10 unique constraints,
  28 CHECK constraints, a *partial* unique index (one active account per
  product), a **deferred constraint trigger** enforcing debits = credits at
  COMMIT, and an audit trigger.
* **Verification:** 0 unbalanced transactions, 0 accounts out of balance, 0
  overdrawn customer accounts, 0 orphaned entries.
* **Negative tests:** seven attempts to write invalid data — six must fail and
  one must succeed; all seven behaved as specified. The important one is a
  single-sided posting, which is accepted at INSERT and rejected at `COMMIT` by
  the deferred trigger — the only way that rule can be expressed.

## 3. Portfolio 2 — Query Processing and Optimisation (7 marks)

Ten queries taken from real HDMB screens and reports, measured before and after
optimisation with `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)`, best of three runs:

| Query | Before | After | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mini statement (most frequent read in the bank) | 17.453 ms | **0.323 ms** | **54× faster** |
| Balance as at a past date | 14.895 ms | **0.168 ms** | **89× faster** |
| Dormancy sweep | 105.757 ms | 40.258 ms | 2.6× faster |
| Branch monthly report | 337.070 ms | **0.234 ms** (materialised) | **1,441× faster** |

Three techniques were applied in increasing cost to the business: indexes,
query rewriting, and a materialised view. The analysis reports what the
optimiser *actually* did, including two cases where my recorded hypothesis was
wrong: a covering index was used at a selectivity where an ordinary index
would have been rejected (because index-only scans remove the random heap
reads the 5–10% rule of thumb is about), and one query correctly got no
benefit from indexing at all because it genuinely needs 67% of the table.
Rewriting the same question three ways produced 68.7 ms (`NOT EXISTS`,
anti-join), 99.7 ms (`LEFT JOIN … IS NULL`), and 541.8 ms (`NOT IN`, 7.9×
slower) — plus a correctness trap where `NOT IN` over a nullable column returns
zero rows silently. Costs are reported too: 61 MB of new indexes against an
81 MB table, and a 480 ms refresh for the materialised view.

## 4. Portfolio 3 — Transactions and Concurrency (7 marks)

Seven experiments, each run as **two or three genuinely concurrent sessions**
orchestrated by a Python harness that timestamps every line of output. A
concurrency anomaly does not exist in a serial script.

The central result is the **lost update**. Account holds NGN 100,000; two
sessions each send NGN 60,000 at the same instant:

* With a read-modify-write transfer (no row lock): **both commit**, two debits
  totalling 120,000 are allowed, the balance ends at 40,000, and
  `account.balance` diverges from the ledger by exactly **NGN 60,000** —
  money created from nothing. The double-entry trigger does not catch it,
  because each transaction individually balances; it is the *ledger* that makes
  the loss detectable at all.
* With `SELECT … FOR UPDATE`: the second session **blocks for 1.2 seconds**,
  wakes when the first commits, re-reads the real balance (40,000) and is
  correctly refused. Reconciliation discrepancy: **0.00**.

Also demonstrated: atomicity with no dirty reads; rollback discarding valid work
done earlier in the same transaction; a non-repeatable read under READ COMMITTED
that disappears under REPEATABLE READ; **write skew** breaching a daily debit
limit under REPEATABLE READ and being refused under SERIALIZABLE with SQLSTATE
40001; a deliberate **deadlock** with the server's own wait-graph output
(SQLSTATE 40P01) and its prevention by ordered lock acquisition; and a
compensating **reversal** that restores balances without deleting history. The
portfolio closes by proving the database was left consistent after the
experiments that deliberately corrupted it, and maps each SQLSTATE to whether
an application should retry it.

## 5. Portfolio 4 — NoSQL and Advanced Data Models (7 marks)

The same HDMB data, reshaped for two access patterns the relational model
serves badly — and loaded by a **real ETL** out of PostgreSQL, not retyped.

* **Document (MongoDB 8):** `customer_profile` (1,988 docs) embeds branch,
  accounts, beneficiaries and the 10 newest postings — the mobile home screen
  as one document; `txn_event` (42,924 docs) embeds each transaction's journal
  legs. Money is stored as `Decimal128` and validated as such, for the same
  reason PostgreSQL uses `NUMERIC`. `recent_activity` is embedded but **bounded
  at 10** by the validator, because the 16 MB document limit is a design
  constraint. Queries include a multikey-index lookup into an embedded array, a
  sort served entirely by a compound index, two aggregation pipelines, a
  pipeline that *writes* (`$merge`, 32,758 edges in 253 ms), and a
  `$graphLookup` money-trail traversal reaching 13 accounts in 4 hops.
* **Key-value (Redis 7):** eight patterns — session tokens, `SET NX`
  idempotency, an **atomic Lua daily-limit script** that solves Portfolio 3's
  write-skew anomaly without any isolation level, a parallel `INCR` test that
  loses nothing where the SQL read-modify-write lost NGN 60,000, a TTL'd balance
  cache, a bounded activity list, and a sorted-set league table.
* **Column-family (Cassandra) and graph (Neo4j)** are presented as *designs*
  with their reasoning, explicitly labelled as modelled rather than deployed.

The analysis is deliberately unflattering where the evidence requires it: the
document read beats the four-join equivalent by **3.379 ms vs ~1–2 ms**, which
is one round trip and simpler application code — not an order of magnitude.
And the validators are shown to accept a document whose debits (5,000) do not
equal its credits (4,000), because `$jsonSchema` cannot express a relationship
*between* array elements. That is why PostgreSQL remains the single system of
record and the NoSQL stores hold derived, rebuildable views.

## 6. Portfolio 5 — Distributed and Cloud Database Exercise (6 marks)

A five-node cluster built by docker-compose, seeded from the same Portfolio 1
schema: one primary, two streaming replicas, two shard nodes.

* **Replication:** both standbys streaming with physical slots; standbys refuse
  writes at the server level; replication lag under a 20,000-row burst measured
  at `replay_lag` ≈ 46 ms.
* **The cost of consistency, measured twice.** A single async→sync→async
  comparison **failed its own control** (the control run came back slower than
  the condition it controlled for), so the number it implied was not reported.
  A repeated-measures benchmark — six alternating rounds, mode verified before
  each — resolved it: asynchronous 1.440 ms against synchronous 2.147 ms,
  **+49.1%**, with non-overlapping ranges.
* **CAP, concretely:** with a *synchronous* standby stopped, a write **blocked**
  — observed in `pg_stat_activity` as `wait_event = 'SyncRep'` — while reads
  continued to be served. The cluster chose consistency over write
  availability, and reverting to asynchronous released the pending commit
  immediately. `statement_timeout` cannot interrupt that wait, which is a
  finding in itself.
* **Failover:** the primary was killed, a standby promoted with `pg_promote()`,
  the timeline incremented to `00000002` (how PostgreSQL prevents split brain),
  0 rows lost — and the *other* standby left orphaned on the old timeline,
  which is the work that makes manual failover slow.
* **Sharding:** `ledger_distributed` is hash-partitioned on `account_id` across
  two nodes via `postgres_fdw`. A query carrying the shard key prunes to **one**
  Foreign Scan (0.681 ms); one without it scans both asynchronously (123.967 ms).
  A predicate using `now()` was **not pushed down** — 41,411 rows were shipped
  and discarded — and replacing it with a literal cut the same query to
  **3.394 ms**, a 36× improvement from pushdown alone.
* **Cloud:** a mapping onto managed PostgreSQL (Multi-AZ + read replica), with
  indicative costs, what a managed service does better, what it does not solve,
  and the conclusion that **HDMB does not need sharding yet** — 550,000 rows
  fit comfortably on one node, and the exercise demonstrates the technique at a
  scale where it is not justified.
